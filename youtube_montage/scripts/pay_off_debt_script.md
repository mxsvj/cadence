# The Minimum Payment Trap (and How to Escape It)

Durée : 4:28 · style doodle · voix Kokoro bf_emma

Sources (vérifiées en octobre 2026) :

- Gal & McShane (2012), Can Fighting Small Battles Help Win the War? Evidence from Consumer Debt Management (Journal of Marketing Research) : https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/academics-research/research/detail/2012/can-fighting-small-battles-help-win-the-war-evidence
- Kettle, Trudel, Blanchard & Häubl (2016), Repayment Concentration and Consumer Motivation to Get Out of Debt (Journal of Consumer Research) : https://www.ualberta.ca/en/folio/2017/01/the-secret-to-paying-off-credit-card-debt.html

Illustrative examples, interest calculated monthly. €3,000 at 20% a year: a fixed €60 a month takes 109 months (about 9 years) and about €3,500 of interest; €150 a month takes 25 months and about €680. A minimum of 2% of the balance (at least €20) would take about 36 years and about €11,200 of interest. Three debts: store credit €800 at 9%, card €2,200 at 21%, car loan €3,500 at 7%; with €300 a month in total, the avalanche pays about €670 of interest over 24 months, the snowball about €750 over 25 months and clears its first debt in month 5 instead of 12. Consolidation: €6,500 at 12% over 3 years costs about €1,270 of interest; at 9% over 6 years about €1,940. Minimum-payment rules differ by country and by card: in France, revolving credit up to €3,000 must be repaid within 36 months; the UK and the Netherlands set their own minimums; many continental cards are charge cards with no minimum at all. Free debt advice: MABS (Ireland), MoneyHelper or StepChange (UK), Schuldnerberatung (Germany, Austria), Point conseil budget (France), Geldfit (Netherlands).

## Texte dit (= sous-titres)

0:00 The minimum payment is the most expensive button in your card app.
0:04 On a €3,000 card, the minimum starts at €60 a month. Keep paying just that, and it takes about nine years. Pay €150, and it's gone in about two.
0:15 That's not because you're bad with money. The lower the minimum, the longer you pay, and the more the lender earns.
0:22 Here's how to escape it, in six steps.

### 1 · Put it all on one page

0:26 One. Put it all on one page.
0:29 Write down every debt: how much you owe, the interest rate, and the minimum payment.
0:34 Include everything: cards, store credit, buy now pay later, car loans, even money you owe friends.
0:41 Most people have never seen the total in one place. It's scary for ten minutes. Then it's a plan.
0:48 And if it feels like too much, free debt advice exists in most countries. Use it.

### 2 · Escape the minimum payment

0:55 Two. Escape the minimum payment.
0:58 Back to that €3,000 card, at 20% a year. At a fixed €60 a month, you pay around €3,500 in interest. More than the debt itself.
1:09 And many real minimums are just a slice of what you owe, like 2%. As the debt shrinks, the payment shrinks too. Pay only that, and the same card can take over thirty years, and about €11,000 in interest.
1:23 The rules vary by country, so check yours.
1:26 At €150 a month, it's about €680 of interest, and two years instead of nine.
1:34 Same debt. Same rate. Seven years of your life back.

### 3 · Pick an order

1:39 Three. Pick an order.
1:42 The avalanche: pay the minimum on everything, and throw every extra euro at the highest interest rate first. Mathematically, it saves the most.
1:51 The snowball: smallest balance first. You close accounts quickly, and each win keeps you going.
1:58 Say you have €300 a month for all three. The avalanche saves about €80 in interest. The snowball clears its first debt in five months, instead of twelve. Both are done in about two years.
2:11 In one study of about six thousand people in a debt programme, those who cleared small accounts first were more likely to clear all of their debt.
2:19 So if the rates are similar, start small. If one rate is much higher, like this card, kill that one first.

### 4 · One debt at a time

2:26 Four. One debt at a time.
2:29 Spreading your extra money thinly across every debt feels fair. But then nothing seems to move.
2:35 Researchers found that people who focused their repayments on one account at a time felt more progress, and paid more aggressively.
2:43 When one is gone, roll its whole payment into the next. Every payoff makes the next one faster.

### 5 · Make the interest smaller

2:50 Five. Make the interest smaller.
2:53 Ask your lender for a lower rate. It doesn't always work, but it costs nothing to ask.
2:59 A consolidation loan can help too, but only if you stop using the old cards. And check the total cost: a longer loan can cost more overall, even at a lower rate.
3:10 Even a few points matter. On €3,000, going from 20% to 10% cuts the interest from about €600 a year to about €300.

### 6 · Don't refill the card

3:22 Six. Don't refill the card.
3:25 Keep a small emergency fund, even €500, so the next surprise doesn't go straight back on the card.
3:32 And when your last debt is gone, keep paying the same amount, into your savings.
3:37 And keep it visible: a list on the fridge, crossed out one line at a time.

### Final message

3:42 Final message.
3:45 Debt isn't a character flaw. It's a maths problem, and maths problems can be solved.
3:50 List it. Pay more than the minimum. Pick an order. One at a time. Lower the rate. Don't refill it.
3:59 That's the whole plan. Screenshot it, and stick it on the fridge.
