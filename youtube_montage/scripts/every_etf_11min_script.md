Même format que la vidéo crypto : 12 chapitres (une « boîte » par type d'ETF), style sombre, couleur par chapitre, voix Emma (Kokoro).
Chaque ligne = un plan. Les chiffres sont vérifiés (sources en bas) ; les formulations prudentes (« about », « around », « more than ») sont voulues.

## Intro

- There are about 16,000 ETFs in the world. Together, they hold around 24 trillion dollars.
- But almost all of them fit into one of twelve boxes.
- Learn the boxes, and you'll know exactly what you're buying. In 11 minutes.
- No hype, and no stock picks. ETFs can lose value. This is education, not financial advice.
- First, what is an ETF? It's a basket of investments. One share gives you a small slice of everything inside.
- ETF stands for exchange-traded fund. It trades on a stock exchange like a normal share, all day long.
- Instead of buying hundreds of shares one by one, you buy a single ETF, and pay one small yearly fee.
- In Europe, most ETFs follow the EU's fund rules, called UCITS. That's why their names often end with those letters.
- UCITS funds keep your investments separate from the company that runs them. If that company goes bust, the investments still belong to the fund.
- American ETFs like VOO usually don't publish the EU's key information document. So most European brokers won't sell them to you.
- Let's open the first box.

## 01 — Index

- Box 1. Index ETFs.
- The biggest box. An index ETF simply copies a list of companies, like the S&P 500 or the MSCI World.
- The MSCI World holds around 1,300 companies, from 23 developed countries.
- But more than two-thirds of it is American companies. The rest is spread across Japan, Europe and others.
- Bigger companies get a bigger weight. When a company grows, its share of the index grows with it, automatically.
- Nobody picks the stocks. A computer follows the list, which is why the fees can be tiny.
- Some S&P 500 ETFs cost as little as 0.03% a year. A typical bank fund often charges around 1.8%.
- Invest €200 a month for 30 years at 7% a year, and that fee gap can cost you more than €60,000.
- Some index ETFs buy the shares directly. That's called physical. Others get the same return through a contract with a bank. That's called synthetic.
- The first ETF launched in America in 1993. Europe got its first ones in 2000, in Frankfurt. Today, it's a giant industry.

## 02 — Country & region

- Box 2. Country and region ETFs.
- Some ETFs zoom in on one part of the world. Europe, Japan, emerging markets, or even a single country.
- Emerging markets, like China, India, Taiwan or Brazil, can offer more growth, but also more risk and bigger swings.
- And when you buy another country, you also buy its currency. A Japan ETF moves with the yen.
- Some investors combine a world ETF with an emerging markets ETF. Others pick one all-world ETF that holds both.
- Many people mostly buy companies from their own country. That's called home bias. But even big European countries are only a few percent of the world's stock market.
- The catch with a single country is concentration. Japan's stock market peaked in 1989, and took 34 years to get back there.

## 03 — Sector

- Box 3. Sector ETFs.
- Sector ETFs hold just one industry. Technology, healthcare, banks, energy, and so on.
- They can soar when their industry is in fashion, and crash when it isn't.
- Energy stocks fell hard in 2020, then became the best performing sector of 2022.
- And if you already own a world ETF, a tech ETF on top means buying the same big companies twice.
- A world ETF already holds every sector. A sector ETF is a bet on just one of them.

## 04 — Thematic

- Box 4. Thematic ETFs.
- Thematic ETFs follow a story. Artificial intelligence, robots, clean energy, cybersecurity.
- They often launch when the story is already hot, and the stocks are already expensive.
- Morningstar found that over 15 years, only about 9% of thematic funds both survived and beat global stocks.
- Many closed, or lagged behind a simple world ETF, partly because of higher fees. A good story isn't always a good investment.

## 05 — Factor

- Box 5. Factor ETFs.
- Factor ETFs, also called smart beta, tilt toward one trait. Cheap stocks, small companies, momentum, or quality.
- There's also equal weight. Every company gets the same slice, instead of letting the giants dominate.
- Academic research found that some of these traits paid a little extra over very long periods.
- But they can lag for years. Value stocks trailed growth stocks for most of the 2010s.
- A factor ETF only works if you have the patience to hold it through the bad years.

## 06 — Dividend

- Box 6. Dividend ETFs.
- Dividend ETFs pick companies that pay out a lot of cash to their shareholders.
- But a dividend isn't free money. When it's paid out, the share price drops by about the same amount.
- A very high yield can even be a warning sign. Sometimes the price has fallen because the business is in trouble.
- In Europe, many ETFs come in two versions. Distributing pays the dividends to you. Accumulating reinvests them automatically.
- Which one suits you depends on how your country taxes them, so check your local rules.

## 07 — Bonds

- Box 7. Bond ETFs.
- Bond ETFs hold loans, to governments or to companies. You earn interest instead of profits.
- Government bonds from strong countries are usually the safest. Company bonds pay more interest, because the risk is higher.
- They usually move less than stocks, which is why many people mix the two.
- When interest rates go up, older bonds lose value, because new bonds pay more.
- But not always. In 2022, interest rates jumped, and global bonds lost about 16%, their worst year on record.
- Short-term bonds move less. Long-term bonds move a lot more when interest rates change.
- Bonds in another currency add currency risk. That's why many bond ETFs come in a version hedged to euros.

## 08 — Money market

- Box 8. Money market ETFs.
- Money market ETFs follow the interest rate that banks pay each other overnight. In euros, it's called €STR.
- Their value climbs slowly, almost every day. It's the closest thing to a savings account in ETF form.
- But it's not a bank account. There's no deposit guarantee.
- When rates are low, they earn almost nothing. When rates were negative, they even lost a little. When rates rise, they earn more.

## 09 — Commodities

- Box 9. Commodities.
- Gold is the classic. But in Europe, a regular fund isn't allowed to hold just one commodity.
- So gold trades as an ETC, an exchange-traded commodity. Often, each share is backed by real gold in a vault.
- Gold has long dry spells too. In dollars, it peaked in 2011, and only got back there in 2020.
- Other commodities, like oil, often use futures contracts. Their returns can drift away from the price you see on the news.
- And commodities pay no interest and no dividends. You only gain if the price goes up.

## 10 — Leveraged & inverse

- Box 10. Leveraged and inverse.
- Leveraged products promise two or three times the daily move of an index. Inverse ones go up when the market goes down.
- The key word is daily. They reset every single day.
- If an index rises 10%, then falls 10%, it's down 1%. A two times leveraged product is down 4%.
- This loss is often called volatility decay. The more the market zigzags, the bigger it gets.
- In a choppy market, they can lose money even if the index ends flat. They're built for short-term traders, not for holding.

## 11 — Active

- Box 11. Active ETFs.
- Active ETFs have a manager who picks the stocks, inside an ETF wrapper.
- They usually cost more, and the manager has to beat the market after those fees.
- According to S&P's long-running research, most active funds lag their index over the long run.
- Some managers do beat it. The hard part is knowing which ones, in advance.

## 12 — Crypto ETPs

- Box 12. Crypto ETPs.
- The last box isn't technically an ETF at all.
- In Europe, a regular ETF can't hold bitcoin directly. So crypto products are ETPs, debt-like notes, often backed by real coins.
- America approved its first spot bitcoin ETFs in January 2024. In Europe, they're still ETPs.
- You get the same wild price swings as crypto, plus the risk of the company that issues the note.
- If you want the full story, our last video explains every crypto, box by box.

## Outro — how to read an ETF name

- Before you buy any ETF, read its name. It tells you the provider, the index, the EU rules, the fund currency, and what happens to the dividends.
- Then check three things. The yearly fee, called TER. The size of the fund. And how closely it follows its index.
- Those are the twelve boxes. Almost every ETF you'll see fits into one of them.
- Remember, ETFs can lose value, and nothing here is a recommendation. This is not financial advice.
- Twelve boxes, eleven minutes. Now, when you see a new ETF, you'll know which box it's in, and what to ask.
