Chaque ligne = un plan. Les chiffres sont vérifiés (sources en bas) ; les formulations prudentes (« about », « often », « usually ») sont voulues.

## Intro

- ISA, ISK, PEA, Depot. Every country in Europe seems to have its own investment accounts, with their own strange letters.
- But almost all of them fit into one of ten boxes.
- Some save you tax. Some lock your money for decades. And some are traps.
- The account you pick can matter as much as what you put inside it.
- Rules change every year and differ by country. This is education, not tax or financial advice.
- Let's open the first box.

## 01 — Savings account

- Box 1. The savings account.
- The simplest one. Your money sits at the bank, earns a little interest, and you can take it out any time.
- In the EU, deposits are protected up to €100,000 per person, per bank, if the bank fails.
- Some countries add tax-free savings accounts, like the Livret A in France, but with a ceiling.
- The catch is inflation. When prices rise faster than your interest, your money quietly loses value.
- A savings account is for money you need soon, not for the next thirty years.

## 02 — Brokerage account

- Box 2. The brokerage account.
- A brokerage account lets you buy shares, ETFs and bonds. No limits, no lock-up, but usually no tax advantage either.
- In Germany, the first €1,000 of investment income each year is tax-free. After that, you pay about 26%.
- In France, gains on a regular account are taxed at 31.4% since 2026.
- And in Ireland, ETFs are taxed at 38%, and every eight years, even if you never sell.
- Most countries tax your gains when you sell. Others, like the Netherlands, tax an assumed return on your savings every year.
- It's the most flexible box. But flexibility usually costs you tax.

## 03 — Tax-free account

- Box 3. The tax-free investment account.
- This is the box many people miss: an investment account with a tax break built in.
- Sweden has the ISK. Britain has the ISA. France has the PEA. Denmark, Finland and Italy have their own versions.
- In Sweden's ISK, you don't pay tax on your gains. You pay a small flat tax on the account's value, about 1% a year. Since 2026, the first 300,000 kronor are tax-free.
- In Britain, you can put up to £20,000 a year into an ISA, and never pay tax on what it earns.
- Denmark's share savings account taxes gains at 17% every year. That's still less than on a normal account.
- In France, after five years in a PEA, your gains skip income tax. You only pay social charges.
- In 2025, the European Commission asked every member state to create accounts like these.
- So if your country has one, it's worth a look. Just check the rules and the limits first.

## 04 — Life insurance

- Box 4. Life insurance.
- In some countries, life insurance isn't really about dying. It's an investment wrapper.
- In France, people hold about two trillion euros in the assurance-vie. After eight years, up to €4,600 of gains you withdraw each year are free of income tax.
- It's also used for inheritance. In France, each beneficiary can receive up to €152,500 without inheritance tax, under conditions.
- Belgium, Italy and Luxembourg have similar contracts, with funds and sometimes ETFs inside.
- The catch is fees. The insurer takes a yearly fee, on top of the funds' own costs.
- A cheap contract can be great. An expensive one quietly eats your returns.

## 05 — Personal pension

- Box 5. The personal pension.
- Personal pension accounts give you a tax break today, in exchange for locking your money until retirement.
- France has the PER. Germany has Riester and Rürup plans. Spain has pension plans, and Poland has the IKE and IKZE.
- Each country sets a limit. In Spain, it's just €1,500 a year for a personal plan.
- The EU even created a pan-European pension, the PEPP, in 2022. Four years later, only two providers offer it.
- The tax break is real. But these accounts are built for money you won't need for decades.

## 06 — Workplace plan

- Box 6. The workplace plan.
- Many employers offer a pension or savings plan, and some add money on top of yours.
- In Germany, if you put part of your salary into a company pension, your employer usually has to add 15%.
- In France, many companies top up what you put into the company savings plan. That top-up is called the abondement.
- Free money from your employer is the closest thing to a guaranteed return. Read your contract.

## 07 — Kids' accounts

- Box 7. Accounts for children.
- Some countries have investment accounts for children. In Britain, the Junior ISA takes up to £9,000 a year.
- Germany plans to put €10 a month into an investment account for every child from 6 to 18, starting in 2027, if parliament approves it.
- Money invested early has the longest time to grow. And time is the one thing kids have plenty of.

## 08 — Robo-advisor

- Box 8. The robo-advisor.
- A robo-advisor invests for you. You answer a few questions, and it picks a mix of ETFs and rebalances it.
- It's convenient. But the service often costs between a quarter and three quarters of a percent a year, on top of the ETFs' own fees.
- Over decades, that fee adds up. You're paying for not having to decide.
- Some robo-advisors work inside a tax-free account or a life insurance contract. So two boxes can stack.

## 09 — Trading account

- Box 9. The trading account.
- This box looks like investing, but it isn't. CFD and leveraged trading accounts let you bet on prices with borrowed money.
- When EU regulators looked at the data, between 74 and 89% of retail accounts lost money.
- That's why these brokers must show that percentage in their ads. If you see it, you know which box you're in.
- Some apps make it feel like a game. Notifications, bright colours, quick trades. That's not an accident.

## 10 — Foreign broker

- Box 10. The foreign broker.
- Many Europeans open an account with a broker in another country, because it's cheaper.
- But a foreign broker usually doesn't handle your home country's taxes. So the paperwork becomes yours.
- In France, forgetting to declare a foreign account can cost €1,500 per account, per year.
- In Germany, you declare the gains yourself in your tax return. Cheaper fees, more homework.
- And Spain asks for a special form if your assets abroad are worth more than €50,000.

## Outro

- Those are the ten boxes. Most people only use two or three of them.
- A common order: a savings cushion first, then any free money from your employer, then a tax-free account if your country has one.
- Rules change every year, and every country is different. Check your tax office's website. This is not financial advice.
- Ten boxes. Now, when someone says ISA, PEA or Depot, you'll know which box it belongs in.
