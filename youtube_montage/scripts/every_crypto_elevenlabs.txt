Box one. Bitcoin. <break time="1.2s" />

Bitcoin is the first cryptocurrency. It launched in two thousand nine, created by someone called Satoshi Nakamoto. <break time="1.2s" />

Nobody knows who Satoshi really is. They disappeared in twenty eleven, and never came back. <break time="1.2s" />

The idea is a public ledger, copied on thousands of computers, where nobody can secretly rewrite the past. <break time="1.2s" />

New blocks are added by miners. They burn electricity to solve a puzzle, and the winner earns new bitcoin. <break time="1.2s" />

And here's the key rule. There will never be more than twenty-one million bitcoin. <break time="1.2s" />

About every four years, the reward for miners is cut in half. This is called the halving. <break time="1.2s" />

The last one, in April twenty twenty-four, cut it to three point one two five bitcoin per block. The very last bitcoin should be mined around twenty-one forty. <break time="1.2s" />

That's why people call it digital gold. Scarce, hard to change, and it doesn't pay you anything. <break time="1.2s" />

But it's volatile. Bitcoin has lost more than seventy percent of its value several times, and recovered each time, so far.

Box two. Ethereum. <break time="1.2s" />

Ethereum launched in twenty fifteen. Its big idea was simple. What if a blockchain could run programs, not just payments? <break time="1.2s" />

These programs are called smart contracts. Once they're live, they run exactly as written, with no company in the middle. <break time="1.2s" />

Every action on Ethereum costs a small fee, called gas, paid in its own coin, ether. <break time="1.2s" />

A lot of what's in the next boxes, stablecoins, DeFi, NFTs, grew up on Ethereum. <break time="1.2s" />

In September twenty twenty-two, Ethereum changed how it works. It stopped mining, and switched to proof of stake. <break time="1.2s" />

Instead of burning electricity, people lock up their ether as a guarantee. Energy use fell by about ninety-nine point nine five percent. <break time="1.2s" />

And one big difference with Bitcoin. Ether has no fixed maximum supply.

Box three. The other blockchains. <break time="1.2s" />

Solana, Cardano, Avalanche, Tron, and many more. Each one is its own blockchain, with its own coin. <break time="1.2s" />

Most of them make the same promise. Faster and cheaper than Ethereum. <break time="1.2s" />

The trade-off is usually decentralisation. Fewer, more powerful computers run the network. <break time="1.2s" />

Solana is the best known example. It's fast and cheap, but its network has gone down several times. <break time="1.2s" />

In twenty seventeen, dozens of coins were sold as the next Ethereum. Many are now barely used. <break time="1.2s" />

So when a new chain promises to change everything, ask one question. Who actually uses it?

Box four. Layer twos. <break time="1.2s" />

Ethereum can get slow and expensive when it's busy. So people built networks on top of it. <break time="1.2s" />

These layer twos collect thousands of transactions, bundle them together, and post the result back on Ethereum. <break time="1.2s" />

The big ones are called Arbitrum, Optimism, and Base, which was built by Coinbase. <break time="1.2s" />

On a layer two, a fee can drop from a few dollars to a few cents. <break time="1.2s" />

Bitcoin has its own version too, the Lightning Network, made for small and fast payments. <break time="1.2s" />

The point is, layer twos aren't a new coin to bet on. They're plumbing.

Box five. Stablecoins. <break time="1.2s" />

A stablecoin is a token that's meant to always be worth one dollar, or one euro. <break time="1.2s" />

How? The company behind it holds real money in reserve, mostly cash and short-term government bonds. <break time="1.2s" />

The two giants are USDT, from a company called Tether, and USDC, from Circle. <break time="1.2s" />

Traders use them to park money between trades, without going back to the bank. <break time="1.2s" />

But not every stablecoin holds real money. In twenty twenty-two, one called Terra USD used an algorithm instead. <break time="1.2s" />

In May twenty twenty-two, it lost its peg. In a few days, about forty billion dollars of value vanished. <break time="1.2s" />

Its sister coin, Luna, fell more than ninety-nine percent. Many people lost their savings. <break time="1.2s" />

Europe reacted. Under Mee-ka, the EU's crypto law, stablecoin issuers have needed authorisation since June twenty twenty-four. <break time="1.2s" />

Tether's USDT doesn't have that authorisation. So the big exchanges removed or restricted it for users in Europe. <break time="1.2s" />

Circle's USDC is authorised. And there are euro stablecoins too, like E-U-R-C.

Box six. Exchange tokens. <break time="1.2s" />

Some crypto exchanges create their own coin. The best known is BNB, from Binance. <break time="1.2s" />

Holding it can give you perks, like lower trading fees. But its value depends a lot on the exchange itself. <break time="1.2s" />

That link can be deadly. Remember FTX? <break time="1.2s" />

In November twenty twenty-two, FTX, one of the biggest exchanges in the world, collapsed in a matter of days. <break time="1.2s" />

Its own token, FTT, crashed by more than ninety percent in a few days. <break time="1.2s" />

Then the truth came out. Around eight billion dollars of customer money was missing. <break time="1.2s" />

Its founder, Sam Bankman-Fried, was later sentenced to twenty-five years in prison. <break time="1.2s" />

So remember. A token tied to one company is only as safe as that company.

Box seven. DeFi, short for decentralised finance. <break time="1.2s" />

It's banking without a bank. Lending, borrowing and trading, all run by smart contracts. <break time="1.2s" />

On Uniswap, you swap one token for another, with no company holding your money. On Ah-vay, you can lend and earn interest. <break time="1.2s" />

Their tokens often give holders a vote on how the app is run. <break time="1.2s" />

The catch? If there's a bug in the code, there's no bank to call, and nobody to refund you. <break time="1.2s" />

And hackers know it. In twenty twenty-two alone, billions of dollars were stolen from crypto projects, much of it from DeFi. <break time="1.2s" />

DeFi is a real experiment in finance without middlemen. Just know there's no safety net.

Box eight. Memecoins. <break time="1.2s" />

It started in twenty thirteen with Dogecoin, a coin made as a joke, with a Shiba Inu dog as its logo. <break time="1.2s" />

Then came Shiba Inu, Pepe, and thousands more, named after dogs, frogs, and internet jokes. <break time="1.2s" />

A memecoin has no product, and no profits. Its price depends on one thing only, attention. <break time="1.2s" />

Today, anyone can create one in about a minute, for almost nothing. Millions have been launched. <break time="1.2s" />

Most of them fade to almost nothing, often within days. Some were built from day one to take your money. <break time="1.2s" />

A few early buyers make a fortune. Most people who buy after the hype lose money. <break time="1.2s" />

So buying a memecoin isn't investing. It's a game, and the odds aren't on your side.

Box nine. NFTs. <break time="1.2s" />

NFT stands for non-fungible token. It's a token that's one of a kind, like a certificate of ownership. <break time="1.2s" />

Usually, the image itself isn't on the blockchain. Often, the token is just a link to it. <break time="1.2s" />

In March twenty twenty-one, an NFT by the artist Beeple sold at Christie's for about sixty-nine million dollars. <break time="1.2s" />

Then the hype faded. NFT trading volumes fell more than ninety-five percent from their twenty twenty-one peak. <break time="1.2s" />

Some uses may survive, like event tickets, or items in video games. <break time="1.2s" />

But most NFTs bought during the hype are now worth a fraction of what people paid.

Box ten. Privacy coins. <break time="1.2s" />

On Bitcoin, every transaction is public. Anyone can follow the money from wallet to wallet. <break time="1.2s" />

Privacy coins hide that. Monero hides who sent the money, who received it, and how much. <break time="1.2s" />

Zee-cash works in a similar way, but there, privacy is optional. <break time="1.2s" />

Privacy has real, legal uses. But regulators worry about money laundering. <break time="1.2s" />

In the EU, new anti-money laundering rules apply from July twenty twenty-seven. <break time="1.2s" />

From then on, regulated crypto platforms won't be allowed to handle privacy coins. <break time="1.2s" />

Several big exchanges already dropped them in Europe. Owning one isn't banned, but buying or selling is getting harder.

Box eleven. Scam coins. <break time="1.2s" />

Some coins exist for one reason only. To take your money. <break time="1.2s" />

The classic is the rug pull. The creators hype a new token, wait for people to buy, then sell everything and vanish. <break time="1.2s" />

In twenty twenty-one, a token named after Squid Game shot up for days. Then buyers noticed they couldn't sell it. <break time="1.2s" />

The creators cashed out, and the price fell to almost zero in minutes. <break time="1.2s" />

And then there's OneCoin. It was sold across Europe as the next Bitcoin, but it never even had a real blockchain. <break time="1.2s" />

Investors put in more than four billion dollars. Its founder disappeared in twenty seventeen, and has never been found. <break time="1.2s" />

The red flags are always the same. Guaranteed returns, pressure to buy fast, a famous face, and a story that's too good to be true. <break time="1.2s" />

If someone promises to double your money, the only thing that doubles is the risk.

Box twelve. The digital euro. <break time="1.2s" />

It's the last box because it's the odd one out. The digital euro isn't crypto at all. <break time="1.2s" />

It would be issued by the European Central Bank. One digital euro would always be one euro, because it is the euro. <break time="1.2s" />

Think of it as digital cash. Like the banknotes in your wallet, but on your phone. <break time="1.2s" />

In December twenty twenty-five, EU governments agreed on their position. In twenty twenty-six, the European Parliament agreed on its own. <break time="1.2s" />

Now they're negotiating the final law. If it passes, the ECB plans tests from twenty twenty-seven, and a possible first launch in twenty twenty-nine. <break time="1.2s" />

So if someone calls the digital euro a crypto, you'll know. It's the opposite. It's money run by a central bank.
