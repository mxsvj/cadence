# Every Bank Fee Explained — voice-over script

Format « boîtes », 10 chapitres, style sombre avec une couleur par chapitre, voix Emma (Kokoro). Durée : 6:33.
Chaque ligne = un plan. Les chiffres sont vérifiés (sources en bas) ; les formulations prudentes (« about », « often », « usually ») sont voulues.

## Intro

- Most people pay bank and investment fees every month without noticing. Each one is small. Together, they're not.
- This video goes through every type of fee, from your bank account and your card to your investments.
- Almost all of them fit into ten boxes.
- Some you can avoid with one click. Some are capped by law. And some are hidden on purpose.
- Prices vary by bank and by country. No brand names here, and this is not financial advice.
- Let's open the first box.

## 01 — Account fees

- Box 1. Account fees.
- Many traditional banks charge a monthly fee just to keep your account open, often bundled with services you never use.
- A few euros a month doesn't feel like much. But €8 a month is almost €100 a year, every year.
- Many online banks offer a basic account for free. It's one of the easiest savings there is.
- And switching is easier than it used to be. Under EU rules, your new bank must offer a switching service that moves your payments for you.

## 02 — Card fees

- Box 2. Card fees.
- Your bank card can come with a yearly fee, and a premium card costs more, with insurance and perks.
- The question is simple: do you actually use those perks? If not, you're paying for a logo.
- For most daily payments, a basic debit card is enough.
- And many people pay for travel insurance twice: once with their card, and once separately.

## 03 — Overdraft fees

- Box 3. Overdraft fees.
- Going below zero is one of the most expensive things you can do at a bank. Interest, plus a fee for every incident.
- In France, the law caps these incident fees at €8 per operation and €80 a month. Many banks charge the maximum.
- A small buffer on your account is often the cheapest way to avoid them.
- And remember, even an authorised overdraft is a loan, often at a high interest rate.

## 04 — Currency fees

- Box 4. Currency fees.
- Pay by card in another currency, and your bank converts the money. Many add a markup on top of the exchange rate.
- Since 2020, EU banks must show you that markup, compared with the European Central Bank's rate.
- Some cards charge no markup at all. On a trip abroad, the difference adds up fast.
- The same thing happens when you buy foreign shares. Your broker may add a markup when it converts your euros.

## 05 — "Pay in euros?"

- Box 5. The question at the cash machine.
- Abroad, a card machine or an ATM may ask: pay in euros, or in the local currency? It sounds helpful.
- It usually isn't. Choosing your home currency lets the machine pick the exchange rate, and it's rarely a good one.
- A Norwegian bank checked 1,500 withdrawals abroad. Choosing the home currency cost more in 99.7% of cases.
- The rule consumer groups give is simple: pay in the local currency.

## 06 — Transfer fees

- Box 6. Transfer fees.
- Sending euros to another EU country must cost the same as sending them at home.
- And since 2025, in the euro area, an instant transfer can't cost more than a normal one.
- Outside the euro, it's different. International transfers can carry fees on both sides, plus a currency markup.
- And since October 2025, your bank checks that the name matches the account before you send. It's called verification of payee.

## 07 — Fund fees

- Box 7. Fund fees.
- Every fund charges a yearly fee, taken quietly from its value. You never see a bill.
- A world index ETF can cost around 0.2% a year. Many bank funds charge 1.5% or more.
- Over decades, that gap compounds, and it can eat a large part of your gains.
- In Europe, every fund sold to the public has a key information document that shows its costs. The cost section is worth two minutes.

## 08 — Entry fees

- Box 8. Entry fees.
- Some funds and insurance contracts charge an entry fee, a few percent of every euro you put in.
- With a 3% entry fee, €1,000 becomes €970 the moment you invest.
- Entry fees are often negotiable, and many online platforms don't charge them at all.
- Some funds also charge an exit fee, or a performance fee when they beat a target.

## 09 — Broker fees

- Box 9. Broker fees.
- Brokers can charge per trade, every year for custody, and for converting currency when you buy foreign shares.
- Some even charge an inactivity fee. You pay for doing nothing.
- Some fees are capped by law. On a French PEA, custody fees are capped at 0.4% a year, and online trades at 0.5%.
- Before choosing a broker, look at the full price list, not just the price of a trade.

## 10 — "Free" trading

- Box 10. Free trading.
- Zero commission apps still earn money. Often through the spread, the gap between the buy and the sell price.
- Some also earned money by sending your orders to a trading firm that paid them for it. That's called payment for order flow.
- The EU banned it. The last national exceptions ended in June 2026.
- A wide spread on every trade can cost more than a small, honest commission.
- Free usually means you pay somewhere else. It's worth finding out where.

## Outro

- Those are the ten fee boxes. Check your last bank statement. You'll probably find two or three of them.
- Once a year, list what you pay: account, card, funds, broker. Then cut what you don't use.
- Prices change, and every bank is different. This is not financial advice.
- Small fees, paid every month, for decades, are never small.
- And every fee you cut is a return you get, guaranteed.
